## Background

A-23 answers: can all consumers on a shared electrical bus meet their demands by their deadlines, jointly?
Several actuators, heaters and computers draw from one battery through one bus with a ceiling. Whether the
design works is a question about a schedule: which consumer draws how much in which tick.

The tempting rule is to add every consumer's peak demand and compare the sum with the bus ceiling. It is wrong
in the direction that throws away good designs. Smallest case: two fingers each need one powered tick, and the
bus can power one finger per tick. With a one-tick deadline no joint schedule exists. With a two-tick deadline
exactly two exist (finger A then finger B, or B then A). A rule that adds both peaks rejects the two-tick
design, which is feasible. The two fingers have identical local summaries; only a contract that carries time
correlation can tell the deadlines apart. So the port contract of a module carries a time-indexed demand, and
this analysis keeps the joint time-indexed problem: exact enumeration when small, a linear program otherwise,
never a sum of peaks. A second rule follows for every pruning analysis: a prune is rejected if any discarded
design is feasible under the same mission.

Tier Solid; exact; in `check`.

## Scope

In: the bus contract as data, the joint feasibility problem, the two solution paths, counting and witnesses,
the dual certificate, findings and repairs, the additive-peak rule as a registered mutant. Out: bus and battery
physics with brownout events (BD-075); the module port contract format (BD-060); thermal sink balance (A-11,
BD-103); the run-time bus reduction kernel (BD-151).

## Technical approach

1. **The bus contract (DESIGN data, read from the model and the task).** A tick length Δ (a rational number of
   seconds on the clock contract), a horizon of T ticks, a capacity C_t per tick (the smaller of the declared
   current or power limit and the source ceiling E²/(4·R) for open-circuit voltage E and series resistance R,
   at the declared state of charge), and for each consumer j: a release tick r_j, a deadline tick d_j, a
   required delivery E_j (energy, or a number of powered ticks), a per-tick draw range [0, p_j] when divisible
   or {0, p_j} when the load is on or off, and optional structure (a minimum run length; precedence "j before
   k"). Regeneration is a negative draw with its own bound.
2. **The feasibility problem.** Find x_{j,t} with x_{j,t} in its draw set, x_{j,t} = 0 outside [r_j, d_j],
   Σ_j x_{j,t} ≤ C_t for every t, and Σ_t x_{j,t}·Δ ≥ E_j for every j, plus the structural constraints.
3. **Path A: exact enumeration** when the number of binary decisions (on-or-off consumers × ticks in their
   windows) is at most 24 (DESIGN default, recorded in the contract). Depth-first over ticks with capacity
   pruning; returns the number of feasible schedules (capped at a declared maximum, with `count_capped`), one
   witness schedule, and for infeasibility the exhausted-search record. Typed `exact`.
4. **Path B: linear program** for divisible loads or larger instances, in exact rationals. For divisible loads
   the program is the problem, and the result is `exact`: a feasible vertex is the witness; infeasibility
   returns a Farkas vector y (Mᵀy ≥ 0, rᵀy < 0 for the standard-form system {x ≥ 0, M·x = r}), checked by two
   exact matrix–vector products. For on-or-off loads beyond the enumeration limit the program is a relaxation:
   infeasibility is a sound exclusion typed `outer`; feasibility of the relaxation proves nothing and the
   result is `unresolved`.
5. **Findings.** `A-23.bus-infeasible` with the binding ticks and consumers (from the dual vector or the search
   record); inside `check` its refusing code is FROB-REF-133 (the bus cannot supply the demand). Repairs ranked
   by the contract and re-solved: raise capacity (`swap-battery`, `set-bus`), reduce a draw (`regear`,
   `swap-actuator`), relax a deadline where the task marks it adjustable. `A-23.tight` (Info) when the number
   of feasible schedules is small, with the count. Both kinds have rows in `check`'s policy (N14bx-01, used by
   BD-108): `A-23.bus-infeasible` refusing with 133, `A-23.tight` reported.
6. **The additive-peak rule** exists only as a registered mutant (`sum-of-peaks`): Σ_j p_j ≤ min_t C_t. The
   conformance suite runs it against the fixtures to show what it would wrongly reject.
7. **Class.** D0, `Tested`; `Certified` when the Farkas certificate is emitted. Budget 100 ms inside stage 6.

## Acceptance criteria

- [ ] Two fingers, unit draw, one powered tick each, capacity one per tick: deadline of one tick gives zero
      schedules and an infeasibility record; deadline of two ticks gives exactly two schedules, both listed.
- [ ] The `sum-of-peaks` mutant rejects the two-tick design (2 > 1) and the conformance run reports the
      discrepancy as the expected catch.
- [ ] Divisible fixture: two loads each needing 1.5 units of energy over three unit ticks with capacity 1 per
      tick are feasible at equality (3 units demanded, 3 available); raising each need to 1.6 units makes the
      instance infeasible, with a Farkas vector that the exact checker accepts.
- [ ] An on-or-off instance above the enumeration limit with an infeasible relaxation is typed `outer`; with a
      feasible relaxation it is `unresolved`, never "feasible".
- [ ] An infeasible bus yields `A-23.bus-infeasible` naming the binding ticks and consumers; `frob analyze
      bus-schedule` reports it with exit 2 (a verb never refuses on a finding). Inside `design.check` the same
      finding stops stage 6 with FROB-REF-133 and exit 3: the registration is BD-108's (its test
      `bus_infeasible_stops_stage6_133`) and the user-side leg is N28a-29's.
- [ ] Contract conformance (reads only bus, battery, actuator-demand and clock fields; D0);
      `frob analyze bus-schedule --json` schema-valid; the fixtures solve inside the 100 ms budget (the first
      four archetype templates, BD-109, are timed by step 9 of N28a-29).

## Test plan

### Unit tests

`crates/frob-analyze/tests/a23_bus.rs`:
- `two_fingers_one_tick_zero`, `two_fingers_two_ticks_exactly_two`.
- `three_fingers_three_ticks_six`: capacity one per tick, three ticks: 3! = 6 schedules. With capacity two per
  tick and a two-tick deadline: 2³ − 2 = 6 schedules (every assignment of three unit jobs to two ticks except
  the two that put all three in one tick), checked against brute force.
- `divisible_feasible_at_equality`, `divisible_infeasible_farkas`.
- `farkas_recheck_exact`: Mᵀy ≥ 0 and rᵀy < 0 in rationals; a perturbed multiplier is rejected.
- `relaxation_typed_outer_or_unresolved`.
- `precedence_and_min_run`: a load that needs two consecutive ticks in a three-tick window sharing with a
  unit load: the witness respects the run.
- `sum_of_peaks_mutant_rejects_feasible`.
- Property: enlarging any deadline or any capacity never turns a feasible instance infeasible; the
  enumeration and the linear program agree on feasibility for every random divisible instance with at most
  12 variables.

## Considerations

- The demand profile is only as good as the task's declaration of when each consumer must act. Where the
  profile is an upper envelope, an infeasibility is not a proof (the true demand may be smaller) and the result
  is `unresolved`; where it is a lower envelope, an infeasibility is `outer`. The contract field says which.
- Bus voltage sag couples the consumers nonlinearly (constant-power loads draw more current as voltage falls).
  The schedule uses the ceiling E²/(4·R) as capacity, which is sound for exclusion; operating-point detail is
  the bus model's.
- This is the standing example of the cascade's prune-soundness audit: pruned designs are sampled and run, and
  one feasible pruned design suspends the pruning rule for the campaign.
