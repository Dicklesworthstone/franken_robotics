## Background

Distributional summaries (the 95th percentile of knee torque, the distribution of disagreement between two
backends) are needed everywhere, and the usual tools for them are mergeable quantile sketches. Their output
depends on insertion and merge order: the same worlds replayed under a different shard split give different
bits, which breaks determinism class D2 for anything computed from them.

The smallest case: a two-centroid sketch that merges its closest pair, fed {0, 3, 5, 9}. In the order 0, 3, 5,
9 it ends as (8/3 with weight 3; 9 with weight 1); in the order 0, 9, 5, 3 it ends as (1.5 with weight 2; 7
with weight 2). Same data, different sketch, so the statistic depends on shard count and arrival.

The rule of the numerics chapter replaces sketches by integer log-bin histograms: the bin is the sign, the
exponent and the top five mantissa bits of the `f32` pattern, so a bin is at most 3.2% wide; counts are
integers; merging is addition; a quantile is reported as a bin interval. Integer counts added in any order
give the same counts, so the histogram is exact, mergeable across shards and identical on every backend. The
price is resolution, and the rule is honest about it: a quantile read from a histogram is an interval, never a
point that was not measured (one of the three wording rules enforced by schema).

This bead is the law, the one octave-splitting bin function of the workspace, and the host type for values
that have no declared quantum. The histogram of a declared telemetry channel, the device kernel that fills it
and the telemetry verb are the telemetry unit's beads (N29a-13, N29a-14, N29a-15); they call this bead's bin
function and obey its laws. Tier: Solid.

## Scope

The program's text describes integer log-bin histograms twice: from the `f32` bit pattern (the numerics rule)
and from a channel value quantized by a declared quantum (the telemetry chapter). Both are built, each in one
bead, on one bin function, and they never compete for the same data (DESIGN, the same statement in N29a-13):

- **A declared telemetry channel is always binned in the quantized form** (N29a-13): everything that
  `K-x-hist` accumulates, that the wide-count drain totals and that `telemetry.histogram` returns. Reason: a
  channel's quantum gives exact bins for small values, a declared range with an overflow bin on each side, and
  bounds that print as exact decimals in the channel's unit; and one channel must never have two histograms
  with different bin edges.
- **The pattern form of this bead (`Hist14`) is for `f32` values that have no declared quantum**: numerics
  diagnostics, residuals and other scale-free quantities reported outside the channel registry. It is never
  stored or returned for a declared channel.
- **Integer quantities** (a distance in units in the last place, a count) use this bead's integer bin function
  directly.

In: the bin function over `f32` patterns; the integer octave bin function and its inverse, implemented once
here (`frob_core::hist::int_bin`, `int_bin_bounds`) and called by N29a-13 for channel histograms and by the
divergence ledger for distances in units in the last place; the histogram type `Hist14` with merge; its
quantiles as intervals; its serialized form; the schema definition of an interval-typed quantile; law tests;
the two-centroid sketch counterexample as a registered negative control.

Out, with the bead that has it:
- the channel histogram: quantization by a declared quantum, the (m, s) layouts with their range and overflow
  bins, 16-bit saturating per-world counts, the quantile query over quantized integers and its canonical
  bytes: N29a-13, which depends on this bead for the bin function.
- the device kernel `K-x-hist`: N29a-14. The verb `telemetry.histogram`: N29a-15.
- device accumulation and the drain handler (BD-207); the drain of 32-bit bins into 64-bit totals (N21c-40);
  the no-wrap bound (N21c-39); channel quanta (the channel declarations of BD-207).

## Technical approach

**Bin function for floating values (DESIGN; `frob_core::hist`).**

  `bin14(x) = bits(x) ≫ 18`   (a 14-bit index: 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, the top 5 of 23 mantissa bits).

There are 16,384 bins. For a normal positive value in [2^e, 2^(e+1)) the bin has absolute width 2^(e−5), so its
width relative to its lower edge is at most 2⁻⁵ = 3.125% (the "at most 3.2%" of the rule). Properties:

1. The bin depends only on the bits of x; no comparison, logarithm or rounding is evaluated.
2. +0.0 is bin 0 and −0.0 is bin 8,192; they are distinct bins and both are reported.
3. Positive bins increase with value; negative bins (index 8,192 and above) increase with magnitude. The value
   order of bins is: negative bins by descending index, then positive bins by ascending index.
4. Infinities and NaNs fall in the bins with exponent 255 (indices 8,160 to 8,191 and 16,352 to 16,383). They
   are counted and reported as `non_finite`, and are excluded from quantiles with their count stated.
5. Subnormals (exponent 0) share bins of absolute width 2⁻¹³¹; the relative-width bound does not apply below
   the smallest normal number, and the interval returned says so by its bounds.
6. Bin bounds: lower = `from_bits(b ≪ 18)`, upper (exclusive) = `from_bits((b + 1) ≪ 18)` for positive finite
   bins; mirrored for negative ones.

**Bin function for non-negative integers (for distances in units in the last place, counts and other integer
quantities).** With sub-bucket exponent s (default 5): for q < 2ˢ the bin is q itself (exact); otherwise with
e = ⌊log₂ q⌋ the bin is ((e − s + 1) ≪ s) | ((q ≫ (e − s)) & (2ˢ − 1)), computed with count-leading-zeros,
shifts and masks. Every bin's width is at most 2⁻ˢ of its lower edge. This is the same octave-splitting rule
the telemetry chapter applies to channel-quantized values; one implementation serves both:

```rust
pub fn int_bin(q: u64, s: u32) -> u32;                      // the index above; total for every q and 0 < s < 32
pub fn int_bin_bounds(b: u32, s: u32) -> (u64, u64);        // inclusive [lower, upper]: for b < 2^s, [b, b];
                                                            // else o = (b >> s) - 1, j = b & (2^s - 1),
                                                            // lower = (2^s + j) << o, upper = lower + 2^o - 1
```

The function knows nothing of a magnitude limit, a sign or an overflow bin: a caller with m magnitude bits
(N29a-13) sends magnitudes below 2ᵐ here, which gives it (m − s + 1)·2ˢ bins per sign, and keeps its own
overflow bins. The two forms agree where both apply: for an integer q with 2ˢ ≤ q < 2²⁴ at s = 5, `int_bin(q, 5)`
and `bin14(q as f32)` name the same interval of values (the octave and the top five bits below the leading
one), and below 2⁵ both give every integer its own bin.

**Histogram.**

```rust
pub struct Hist14 { counts: BTreeMap<u16, u64> }           // sparse, ordered; integers above 2^53 serialize as strings
impl Hist14 {
    pub fn add(&mut self, x: f32);                          // counts[bin14(x)] += 1
    pub fn merge(&mut self, other: &Hist14);                // integer addition per bin
    pub fn total(&self) -> u64;
    pub fn quantile(&self, q_num: u64, q_den: u64) -> Result<QuantileInterval, QuantileError>;  // q = q_num/q_den
}
pub struct QuantileInterval { lower: f32, upper: f32, bin: u16, rank: u64, n: u64, excluded_non_finite: u64 }
```

`merge` is commutative and associative because it is integer addition; a histogram built from any partition
of a multiset of values, merged in any order, equals the histogram built from the whole.

**Quantile.** For q in (0, 1], given as an exact rational q_num/q_den of two `u64` with 0 < q_num ≤ q_den
(DESIGN: `frob-core` is layer 0 and cannot name the exact rational of the host exact kernel, which N13-10
specifies in `frob-lie`; the rank needs one 128-bit product and nothing more): n = the count of finite samples;
rank r = max(1, ⌈q·n⌉) = max(1, (q_num·n + q_den − 1) div q_den), computed in `u128`; walk the bins in value
order accumulating counts until the running total reaches r; return that bin's bounds as the half-open interval
[lower, upper), with r and n. With n = 0 the call refuses (there is no quantile of nothing), and so does a q
outside (0, 1]: both are values of the module's own `QuantileError { Empty, BadFraction }`, which the caller that
reports them maps to its refusal (no refusal code is named in this module). `QuantileInterval` has no method that returns a single number:
a consumer that wants a point must choose a bound and say which.

**Serialized form (`frob.hist14/1`).** `{ "bins": [[index, "count"], …] in ascending index, "non_finite":
"count", "n": "count" }`; counts are decimal strings (they may exceed 2⁵³). A quantile is serialized as
`{ "lower": …, "upper": …, "bin": …, "rank": "…", "n": "…" }`. The envelope schema's definition
`QuantileInterval`, which the wording rules (N35-28) require wherever a quantile comes from a histogram, admits
exactly two object shapes and no scalar: this one, for a `Hist14`; and the channel-histogram shape of N29a-13
as `telemetry.histogram` prints it (N29a-15), `{ "p", "rank", "interval": [lo, hi], "bin": [q_lo, q_hi],
"count_in_bin", "exact", "overflow" }`, with bounds as exact decimal strings in the channel's unit and the
sample count and the saturation flag in the enclosing histogram object (`count`, `saturated`). Each shape
carries both bounds of an interval (or one bound and an `overflow` mark) and the rank, and has the sample
count beside it; a scalar in place of either fails validation.

Non-finite samples differ by form, on purpose: a `Hist14` counts them in the exponent-255 bins and excludes
them from quantiles with their count stated; on a declared channel a NaN faults its world and enters no bin,
and an infinity saturates into the channel's overflow bin (N29a-14, N29a-13).

**Worked case.** {0, 3, 5, 9}: patterns 0x00000000, 0x40400000, 0x40A00000, 0x41100000; bins 0, 4,112, 4,136,
4,164; one count each, in either insertion order. Bin bounds: [3.0, 3.0625), [5.0, 5.125), [9.0, 9.25). The
median (q = ½, r = 2) is the interval [3.0, 3.0625); the 75th percentile (r = 3) is [5.0, 5.125).

## Acceptance criteria

- [ ] `bin14` equals `bits ≫ 18` for all 2³² patterns (exhaustive test), and bin bounds contain their value for every finite pattern.
- [ ] For every positive normal bin, (upper − lower)/lower ≤ 2⁻⁵ exactly computed (checked for all 8,128 positive normal bins).
- [ ] The histogram of {0, 3, 5, 9} is identical in the orders (0, 3, 5, 9) and (0, 9, 5, 3): bins 0, 4,112, 4,136 and 4,164 with count 1 each.
- [ ] Merge is order-independent: for 10⁴ seeded cases, a multiset of 1,000 values split into 1 to 8 parts and merged in a random order gives the histogram of the whole, bin for bin.
- [ ] `quantile` returns intervals, with the worked values above; it refuses on an empty histogram; non-finite samples are excluded and counted.
- [ ] The type exposes no scalar quantile, and the definition `QuantileInterval` rejects a scalar. (That the envelope schema uses the definition wherever a quantile comes from a histogram, so that a scalar there fails validation, is the wording rule of N35-28, which depends on this bead.)
- [ ] The integer bin function at s = 5 maps 0 to 31 exactly, and its bins above have relative width at most 2⁻⁵ (checked for all q below 2²⁴).
- [ ] `int_bin_bounds` is the inverse of `int_bin`: for every bin b of s = 5 below 2²⁴ and of s = 3 below 2¹⁶, `int_bin(lower) = int_bin(upper) = b` and `int_bin(upper + 1) = b + 1`.
- [ ] One bin function: `frob-chronicle` and `frob-kernels` hold no second octave-splitting index (the host side calls `int_bin`; a device kernel cannot call host code, so the dialect twin inside `K-x-hist` is held to the host histogram, and through it to this function, by that kernel's differential check in N29a-14); for every integer q with 32 ≤ q < 2²⁴, `int_bin(q, 5)` and `bin14(q as f32)` have equal value bounds.
- [ ] The schema definition `QuantileInterval` accepts the `Hist14` shape and the channel-histogram shape, and rejects a scalar and an object with one bound and no `overflow` mark.
- [ ] The two-centroid sketch control produces two different summaries for the two orders, and the registered negative control records it.

### Known answers and negative controls
- KA-800: the set {0, 3, 5, 9} histogrammed in two orders gives identical histograms. Negative control: the
  closest-pair two-centroid sketch.
- The telemetry chapter's own smallest case for order dependence: a compactor of capacity two that keeps the
  lower of each sorted pair summarizes 1, 2, 3 as {1 with weight 2, 3} and 3, 2, 1 as {2 with weight 2, 1},
  medians 1 and 2; integer bin counts are 1, 1, 1 in both orders.
- Metamorphic: permuting the samples, or re-partitioning them into shards, leaves the histogram unchanged.
- Determinism lanes: the type is integer arithmetic (class D0): identical twice, across worker counts, build
  profiles and CPU architectures.

## Considerations

- Two parametrizations appear in the program's text: bins taken from the `f32` pattern (this chapter) and
  bins of a channel value quantized by a declared quantum with 2ˢ sub-buckets per octave (the telemetry
  chapter; defaults 24 magnitude bits and s = 5 for run-level histograms, 16 bits and s = 3 for per-world
  ones). Both are integer-count histograms with the same octave-splitting rule; this bead implements the
  pattern form and the integer bin function and leaves the quantum, the layouts and the device kernel to the
  telemetry beads. Which governs where is settled in Scope (DESIGN): the quantized form for every declared
  channel, the pattern form only for values without a quantum. At s = 5 both have the same resolution, a bin
  at most 2⁻⁵ = 3.125% of its lower edge; the telemetry chapter prints it as 3.1% and the numerics rule
  bounds it as "at most 3.2%", and neither is a different number.
- A 3% bin is coarse for a tail percentile of a narrow distribution. The repair is a channel quantum chosen
  for the channel, not interpolation inside a bin.
- Per-world histograms on devices use narrower counters with saturation; saturation is reported, never
  silent.
