## Background

The program has two machines with different jobs. One is a large-memory Mac (36 CPU cores, an 80-core GPU, 256 GB
of unified memory): it holds the world bank of compiled scenes, runs the referee and replay, does derivation and
proof work, and serves the viewer. The other, `trj`, has two RTX 4090 cards and is the training tier. They are not
a fast GPU and a slow GPU. What crosses between them is deliberately small: seeds and scene recipes, design and
module differences, model-variant tables, policy weights, receipts, digests and telemetry summaries. Compiled
scene fields, world state and rollout buffers never cross; a scene is regenerated on the receiving machine from
its seed and must arrive at the same identity.

A **peer link** between the two daemons would let one queue assign jobs to either machine (the fleet scheduler, a
Frontier mechanism). It has upkeep: a second protocol surface to secure and test, failure modes when a machine
drops out, and a standing temptation to move bulk data. Its ceiling was stated before any test: adding the Mac's
GPU to training adds at most its share of the pair of cards (0.125 at a quarter of one card's throughput, 0.30 at
60%), so the gain cannot come from training throughput. It can come only from moving non-training work off the
training cards, and removing a fraction g of the work on the critical machine speeds a campaign by at most
1/(1 − g): a factor of 1.5 needs g ≥ 1/3.

OPEN-TETHER-5 asks the owner whether the link earns that upkeep. Fail-closed default: **standalone machines.**
What the default costs: the fleet scheduler assigns jobs by the role table only (applied through ordinary job
verbs on each machine), and no cross-machine queue exists. Needed by M4, when the fleet scheduler's gate is due.

## Scope

In: the decision record; the evidence page (the pilot's number and the kill gate's state); what each answer
switches; the checks that the standalone default is in force. Out: the fleet scheduler, its pilot and KILL-FLEET
(their own beads); the daemon; the seeds-only transfer rule and its refusal (the device layer).

## Technical approach

**Evidence comes first.** This is an owner question about upkeep, asked after a measurement: the pilot records a
phase ledger of one design campaign on `trj` alone and reports g, the share of wall-clock that is not training.
Kill gate KILL-FLEET fires if g < 1/3, or if after one repair cycle the paired lower bound of campaign speedup
is still below 1.3 against the better single machine; its fallback is exactly the default here (standalone
machines, jobs assigned by the role table through ordinary job verbs, the seeds-only rule kept). So:

- if KILL-FLEET has fired, the question is closed by the kill-gate ADR and this bead records the default as
  following from it;
- if the pilot shows g ≥ 1/3 and the gate's speedup holds, the owner is asked whether to keep the link running.

**What is put to the owner at the M3 exit:** g from the pilot with its ledger; the measured or projected campaign
speedup with its interval; the link's upkeep (the protocol surface, the authentication between the machines, the
chaos tests it adds, the per-campaign transfer budget of at most 1 GB per campaign-hour); and the two answers.

| Answer | Effect |
|---|---|
| Standalone (default) | Each machine runs its own daemon and queue. An operator or agent submits to the machine the role table names. `fleet.plan` prints the role-table assignment as advice and dispatches nothing across machines. The peer listener is not started. |
| Keep the link | The daemons authenticate to each other; `fleet.plan` assigns by role affinity, then shortest estimated work with ageing; a machine that drops out has its jobs re-queued on the other only where role and class allow; transfers are metered per class and a field or state slab is refused. |

**Recording.** ADR `docs/adr/ADR-PROG-12-peer-link.md`; a configuration key `fleet.peer_link = false | true` in the
daemon's configuration, generated from the ADR; the register row moves to `decided` or `defaulted`.

**Checks.** With `peer_link = false`: no daemon listens on the peer port (a socket scan in the test); a job
submitted with a target on the other machine is refused with the repair "submit on that machine"; `fleet.status`
reports `standalone`. With `true`: the link's own gate must have a recorded PASS, otherwise the configuration is
refused (a link cannot be switched on by decision alone).

## Acceptance criteria

- [ ] The register row is `decided`, `defaulted` or closed by the KILL-FLEET ADR at the M4 exit; the exit record
      says which, with g.
- [ ] Under the default no peer listener exists, cross-machine submission is refused with its repair, and
      `fleet.status` reports `standalone`.
- [ ] `peer_link = true` without a recorded PASS of the fleet gate is refused.
- [ ] A comparison whose arms ran on different device families is still reported as unpaired unless bit equality
      across backends holds, under either answer.
- [ ] The evidence page is generated from the pilot's ledger, not typed.

## Test plan

### Unit tests
`crates/frob-conformance/tests/open_tether5.rs`: `default_is_standalone`; `no_peer_listener_when_false`;
`cross_machine_submit_refused_with_repair`; `link_on_requires_gate_pass`; `kill_fleet_fired_closes_question`;
`speedup_ceiling_formula` (g = 1/3 gives 1.5; g = 0.2 gives 1.25).

### End-to-end script and logging
`scripts/e2e/owner_tether5.sh`: starts one daemon with the default configuration, asserts the absence of the peer
listener and the `standalone` status, submits a job naming the other machine and asserts the refusal; then sets
`peer_link = true` on a scratch configuration with no gate record and asserts the configuration refusal. Logs:
`cmd`, exit codes, the configuration digest, `refusal` lines, a `summary`.

### Known answers and negative controls
Known answers: 1/(1 − g) at g = 1/3 is 1.5; the training-share ceilings 0.125 and 0.30; the transfer target of 1 GB
per campaign-hour. Negative controls: a peer listener found under the default; a link switched on without its
gate; a state slab offered for transfer (refused by the device layer whatever the answer).

## Considerations

- Two nodes do not need a clever scheduler; the question is whether a link is worth securing and testing at all.
  The default is cheap and loses nothing that the role table does not already give.
- Security: the peer link is the only network surface between machines; under the default there is none to attack.
- If a third machine is ever added the question reopens with a new identifier; this one is tombstoned.
